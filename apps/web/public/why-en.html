Why individuals and organisations need a knowledge base, and who does

As material piles up, memory and folders stop being enough. A knowledge base solves one problem: keep scattered material in one place, so that one question finds it, the answer can be checked, and only the right people can see it. This page explains why individuals and organisations each need one, who needs one most, and what Quberi can and cannot do today.

Why an individual needs a knowledge base

The material is scattered: you know it exists, but can't find it

Notes, PDFs, screenshots in chats, saved web pages and email attachments live in different apps. When you need one, you only remember having seen it, and searching takes ages. A knowledge base keeps them in one place and finds them by meaning, so you don't have to remember which folder a file is in.

A general-purpose AI doesn't know your material

Ask a general-purpose AI and it doesn't know how your contract is worded, where your project stands or what is in your reading notes. It gives general answers and sometimes makes things up. Put the material in a knowledge base and let the AI answer only from it, with sources shown, and the answer becomes usable and can be checked afterwards.

The more you accumulate, the more it costs to go back

The more you have read and done, the harder it is to retrieve any of it. A knowledge base lets you ask for what you organised earlier at any time.

Privacy should be yours to decide

Personal material often includes income, contracts, medical records and client information. What an individual needs is that only they can see it, that they can look at what is sent to a large model before it goes, and, where necessary, that it can stay on their own computer.

Which individuals need one

  • Students and researchers: papers, lecture notes and references are spread around, and writing means digging through them again and again. One question finds the relevant passage, and you can go back to the original to check.
  • Freelancers and consultants: several clients at once, each with a pile of contracts, requirements and meeting notes. One space per client keeps the material apart, so it never gets mixed up.
  • Lawyers, accountants, advisers and other professionals: plenty of rules, cases and contract templates, plus clients' confidential material. Answers come with sources, so they can be checked, and the material is visible only to you.
  • Engineers and developers: technical documents, designs, lessons learned and manuals, forgotten as soon as they are written. Turn your own records into something you can ask questions of, and connect it to the AI assistants you already use.
  • Content creators: topics, source material, interview records and references keep growing. Keep each subject in its own space and ask while you write.
  • Exam candidates and self-learners: textbooks, past papers, wrong answers and notes. One space per subject; ask and revise against your own material.

What they share: the material is built up over time, you often need to go back to it, and you want it to belong only to you.

Why an organisation needs a knowledge base

Knowledge stays in people's heads and leaves with them

Processes, experience and the history behind a client are often clear to only a few people. When one leaves or moves, the thread breaks. A knowledge base writes it down and keeps it in one place, and when someone leaves, their access can be taken back in one action.

New people take long to get up to speed, and veterans keep being interrupted

The same questions come up again and again: how to claim expenses, what this client requires, what the parameter is for this step. With a knowledge base, newcomers ask it first, and veterans only deal with the questions that really need a person.

Many files, messy versions, no way to know which one is right

The same policy exists in several versions: one in email, one on the shared drive, one in a chat. In a knowledge base a document can have clear versions, old versions are kept, and the answer says which version it came from.

When using AI, the worry is overstepping and wrong answers

An organisation that wants to use AI worries: will the AI tell someone something they have no permission to see? Is the answer right, and does it have a source? Has material been sent somewhere it shouldn't go? An enterprise knowledge base has to answer: who can see what, can an answer be checked, and can a problem be traced.

The organisation is responsible for where its material goes

Which material went to which large model, who asked what, who changed whose access: an organisation needs to be able to account for these. An audit trail is what makes that possible.

Which organisations need one

  • Manufacturing and engineering: drawings, process cards, quality-system documents, equipment nameplates and manuals, many of them scans and pictures. Text in scanned PDFs and pictures can be read and searched, with spaces by client and by project.
  • Organisations with many policy documents (administration, HR, finance): many policies, procedures and templates, and staff keep asking the same questions. Staff ask for themselves, answers come with sources, and repeated questions go down.
  • Project- and client-based firms (implementation, pre-sales, consulting, advertising): each client has a pile of material, project teams change often, and one client's material must not be visible to another's team. One space per client, access granted by project team; change the person, change the access.
  • Technical service, customer service and after-sales teams: many product manuals, fault cases and upgrade notes, and replies must be fast and accurate. Ask against the manuals and cases, and check the answer against the original.
  • Growing small and mid-sized companies: run on a few key people passing things on by word of mouth, and anything breaks when they move. Capture experience so that newcomers get up to speed faster.
  • Industries with sensitive material (finance, healthcare, law, research-heavy work): material can't be sent outside casually, nor shown to everyone. Access down to a single document, self-hosting, and what goes to a large model can be checked first.
  • Government departments and public institutions: many policy documents, official papers, service procedures, internal notices and meeting materials; confidentiality requirements are high, and the material usually can't be put on an outside service. Quberi can be privately deployed in your own data centre or servers, so documents, indexes, conversations and audit logs stay in your own environment; connect a local model you host yourself and your questions and the retrieved passages don't leave your network either; access goes down to a single document, with a record of every step. For the specific deployment, contact Quberi; see "What to know up front" below.

What they share: lots of material, people who change, requirements on who can see what, and a wish to use AI without losing control.

What Quberi can do in these situations

  • Personal and enterprise knowledge bases, both: sign up and you get a personal knowledge base; from the same account you can create an enterprise organisation, or join someone else's and switch between them.
  • Answers with sources: every answer names the documents it used, with page, line and version where available, and one click takes you back to the original; if it isn't in the material, the AI says it found nothing.
  • Two layers of access, down to a single document: first open a space to departments, positions, groups or individuals, then hide or share a single document separately. The AI only draws on documents the person asking is already allowed to read. See document-level permissions (in Chinese).
  • Offboarding in one action: web sign-in, connected AI tools and Feishu sync stop, any model keys the person entered are wiped, and their personal space is frozen.
  • Audit trail: admins can see every question asked, which documents the AI drew on, and every change of access.
  • Check before sending to a large model: once signed in, turn on "Check before sending" to look at, and edit, what goes out before it goes.
  • Reads scans and the text in pictures: with local text recognition, without sending it to a third party.
  • Connect the AI assistants you already use: ChatGPT, Claude, Claude Code, Cursor, Codex and others can connect, with the same access as the person; off by default, turned on by an organisation admin.
  • Self-host and choose your model: use Quberi's cloud, or run it on your own computer, in your data centre or in your own cloud account; use the built-in models or connect one you host yourself. The code is open source (AGPL-3.0).
  • A sign-in method that fits you: every organisation's identity system is different, so Quberi would rather adapt the sign-in method to each customer's actual needs; contact Quberi.

What to know up front

Self-hosting is at an early stage today: you set it up yourself with Docker Compose, with no ready-made images; a setup that uses only a local chat model has not been thoroughly tested; and it is not an air-gapped environment, because email sign-in codes need to send mail, Google and Feishu sign-in need to reach those services, and the first download of the embedding model needs internet access.

For government departments and organisations handling classified material: private deployment requires contacting Quberi for the options; there is currently no published statement of security certification, graded-protection or confidentiality qualification; and if you need a fully offline internal network (for example a physically isolated classified network), evaluate it with Quberi first.

The built-in sign-in methods today are email code, Google and Feishu, and apart from Feishu there is no built-in enterprise single sign-on (SSO); Quberi adapts it to your needs when required. Syncing from WeCom, DingTalk and cloud drives / SharePoint is planned and not available yet. Picture search is not tuned for engineering drawings, and searching a very short part number on its own may find nothing, while adding context works. How a cloud model keeps content and whether it trains on it depends on the provider you choose, and Quberi cannot promise it on their behalf. We write these down instead of hiding them.
